## Big Bang Theory
- The universe began as an infinitely dense, hot singularity approximately 13.8 billion years ago, based on Planck satellite measurements.
- Initial rapid expansion, known as cosmic inflation, caused the universe to grow by a factor of at least 10^26 in less than 10^-32 seconds.
- The first simple atomic nuclei (hydrogen, helium, lithium) formed within the first 3–20 minutes after the initial expansion during Big Bang nucleosynthesis.
- The cosmic microwave background radiation, discovered in 1965, is the cooled remnant (2.725 K) of the initial hot glow, dating to 380,000 years after the Big Bang.
- Ongoing expansion is evidenced by Hubble's Law: galaxies recede at velocities proportional to their distance (Hubble constant ≈ 70 km/s/Mpc).

## Core Ideas
- The universe began as an infinitely dense and hot singularity approximately 13.8 billion years ago.
- A period of exponential expansion, known as cosmic inflation, occurred within the first fraction of a second after the initial event.
- The first simple atomic nuclei (hydrogen, helium, lithium) formed within the first 3 to 20 minutes during Big Bang nucleosynthesis.
- The cosmic microwave background radiation, a remnant glow from 380,000 years after the Big Bang, provides a snapshot of the early universe.
- The observed redshift of distant galaxies, indicating the universe is expanding, serves as primary observational evidence for the theory.

## How To Learn
- **Engage with the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) Data**: Analyze Planck satellite data (e.g., temperature fluctuations of 2.725 K ± small anisotropies) to understand primordial universe conditions.
- **Calculate Hubble’s Law with Real Numbers**: Use observed redshifts and distance measurements (e.g., Hubble constant H₀ ≈ 73 km/s/Mpc) to compute recession velocities of galaxies.
- **Simulate Nucleosynthesis**: Model the first 3 minutes to predict light element abundances (e.g., ~75% hydrogen, ~25% helium by mass).
- **Study Inflationary Epoch Models**: Examine how exponential expansion (e.g., factor of 10²⁶ in 10⁻³² seconds) resolves flatness and horizon problems.
- **Practice Deriving Friedmann Equations**: Solve for scale factor a(t) in different universe models (matter-dominated, radiation-dominated, Λ-dominated).

## Examples
- **Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)**: A faint glow of radiation at 2.725 K, observed uniformly across the sky, providing direct evidence of the universe's hot, dense state approximately 13.8 billion years ago.
- **Abundance of Light Elements**: Predicts that about 25% of the universe's mass should be helium-4, with trace amounts of deuterium (∼0.002%) and lithium-7 (∼10⁻¹⁰), matching observed primordial abundances.
- **Hubble's Law**: Galaxies recede at velocities proportional to their distance (e.g., a galaxy 1 Mpc away moves at ∼70 km/s), indicating uniform expansion from a single point.
- **Large-Scale Structure Formation**: Tiny density fluctuations (on the order of 1 part in 100,000) in the early universe grew via gravitational instability to form galaxies, clusters, and cosmic voids.

## Summary
- The universe began as a singularity approximately 13.8 billion years ago, expanding from an infinitely hot and dense state.
- Within the first 10⁻³² seconds, the universe underwent a period of exponential expansion known as cosmic inflation.
- The first protons and neutrons formed within the first 3 minutes during the era of Big Bang nucleosynthesis.
- The cosmic microwave background radiation, a remnant from 380,000 years after the Big Bang, provides a snapshot of the early universe.
- The expansion of the universe is accelerating due to dark energy, which constitutes about 68% of the universe's total energy content.
