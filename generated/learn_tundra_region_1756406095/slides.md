## Tundra Region
- Defined by permafrost, a layer of permanently frozen subsoil extending 10 inches to over 3,000 feet deep, which prevents deep-rooted tree growth.
- Receives only 6 to 10 inches of annual precipitation, classifying it as a cold desert despite its saturated surface conditions in summer.
- Supports low biodiversity with approximately 1,700 species of vascular plants and 48 land mammal species adapted to extreme cold.
- Experiences two main seasons: a long, harsh winter with temperatures averaging -30°F and a short, cool summer averaging 37–54°F.
- Characterized by a brief growing season of 50 to 60 days, during which the top layer of permafrost thaws, creating bogs and shallow lakes.

## Core Ideas
- Permafrost underlies 85% of the tundra, with an active layer thawing to just 15–50 cm (6–20 in) deep in summer.
- Vegetation is dominated by low-lying plants: mosses, lichens, sedges, and dwarf shrubs less than 30 cm (1 ft) tall.
- Average annual temperatures range from -34°C to -6°C (-30°F to 21°F), with a growing season of only 50–60 days.
- Fauna is adapted for extreme cold, including species like the Arctic fox, caribou (with herds migrating over 5,000 km), and lemming.
- The region stores vast amounts of carbon—estimated at 1,400 gigatons—primarily locked in frozen soil.

## How To Learn
- **Engage with permafrost depth data**: Analyze soil temperature profiles from boreholes; for example, in Utqiaġvik, Alaska, permafrost extends over 300 meters deep, but active layer thickness varies annually by ±15 cm.
- **Track phenological shifts**: Monitor plant flowering and animal migration dates using satellite imagery and field journals; note that dwarf birch leaf-out now occurs 5–7 days earlier per decade due to warming.
- **Quantify carbon flux**: Use chamber measurements to record CO₂ and CH₄ emissions from tundra soils; typical summer methane fluxes range from 5–50 mg CH₄/m²/day in wet sedge meadows.
- **Map microtopography with drones**: Capture 5-cm resolution imagery to classify patterned ground features like ice wedge polygons, which can expand by 1–3 cm/year due to thaw.
- **Correlate NDVI with ground truthing**: Compare satellite-derived Normalized Difference Vegetation Index values with in-situ plant cover surveys; a 0.1 increase in NDVI often corresponds to 15% greater shrub encroachment.
- **Simulate thaw scenarios**: Run soil thermal models (e.g., GIPL) using IPCC climate projections; predict active layer deepening by 20–40% under RCP 4.5 by 2050.

## Examples
- **Permafrost depth**: Continuous permafrost extends 400-700 meters deep in northern Alaska, with active layer thaw reaching 0.3-1 meter annually.
- **Arctic fox adaptation**: Changes fur color seasonally (white in winter, brown in summer) and can survive at -50°C due to dense fur and compact body shape.
- **Tundra carbon storage**: Contains approximately 1,400 gigatons of organic carbon—nearly double the carbon in Earth's atmosphere.
- **Plant survival strategy**: Dwarf willow (Salix herbacea) grows horizontally less than 6 cm tall to avoid wind damage while extending roots 2 meters laterally.
- **Pingos formation**: Ice-cored hills up to 70 meters high form through groundwater hydrostatic pressure in drained lake basins, like those in Canada's Tuktoyaktuk Peninsula.

## Summary
- Covers approximately 20% of Earth's land surface, primarily in the Arctic Circle, with smaller alpine tundra regions on high mountains worldwide.
- Characterized by permafrost, a layer of permanently frozen subsoil extending hundreds of meters deep, which prevents water drainage and creates marshes in summer.
- Average annual temperatures range from -12°C to -6°C, with a short growing season of just 50-60 days and low annual precipitation of 15-25 cm, classifying it as a cold desert.
- Vegetation is dominated by low-lying plants like mosses, lichens, sedges, and dwarf shrubs (e.g., Arctic willow), with no trees due to frozen soil and harsh winds.
- Supports specialized fauna including caribou (reindeer), Arctic foxes, lemmings, and migratory birds, many of which have adaptations like thick fur or seasonal white camouflage.
- Highly vulnerable to climate change, with current warming rates approximately twice the global average, leading to permafrost thaw and significant ecosystem alteration.
