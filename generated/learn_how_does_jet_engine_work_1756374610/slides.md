## How a Jet Engine Works: An Overview
- **Jet propulsion**: thrust generated by expelling high-speed exhaust gases
- Core principle based on Newton's Third Law: for every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction
- Main components: intake, compressor, combustion chamber, turbine, and exhaust

Together, they transform fuel into powerful forward motion, allowing aircraft to soar through the skies with precision and force. -->

## The Combustion Process
- **Combustion chamber**: where fuel is injected and mixed with compressed air
- Ignition: initially sparked, then self-sustaining due to high temperatures and continuous fuel-air mix
- **Stoichiometric ratio**: ideal fuel-to-air mixture for complete combustion, though engines often run leaner

<!-- NARRATION: Now, let's explore the combustion chamber, where the real action happens! Here, fuel mixes with compressed air and is ignited by a spark. Once started, the fire keeps burning due to the high heat and constant fuel supply. Engineers aim for the perfect fuel-to-air mix, called the stoichiometric ratio, though often they use a bit less fuel to improve efficiency and control the intense energy release. -->

## The Exhaust and Thrust Generation
- **Exhaust nozzle**: accelerates gases to high speeds, converting thermal energy to kinetic energy
- Thrust equation: \( \text{Thrust} = \dot{m} \times (V_e - V_0) \), where \( \dot{m} \) is mass flow rate, \( V_e \) is exhaust velocity, \( V_0 \) is aircraft speed
- **Afterburner** (optional): injects additional fuel into exhaust for extra thrust, used in military aircraft

<!-- NARRATION: The exhaust stage is where thrust is born! Gases rush out of the nozzle at incredible speeds, turning heat into motion. The thrust equation shows it depends on mass flow and speed differences. Some jets, like military fighters, have an afterburner that adds extra fuel for a boost, making them roar with even more power—perfect for high-speed maneuvers and quick takeoffs! -->

## Key Performance Metrics
- **Thrust-to-weight ratio**: critical for acceleration and climb performance
- **Specific fuel consumption (SFC)**: measures fuel efficiency; lower SFC is better
- **Bypass ratio**: in turbofans, ratio of air bypassing the core to air passing through; higher ratios improve efficiency

<!-- NARRATION: How do we measure jet engine performance? Key metrics include thrust-to-weight ratio for agility, specific fuel consumption for efficiency, and bypass ratio in turbofans. A high bypass ratio means more air bypasses the core, saving fuel and making engines quieter and greener. These numbers help engineers design engines that are powerful, economical, and reliable for all types of aircraft. -->

## Efficiency and Environmental Considerations
- **Thermal efficiency**: improved via higher compression ratios and turbine inlet temperatures
- **Emissions**: nitrogen oxides (NOx), carbon dioxide (CO2), and particulates; reduced via lean-burn combustion and alternative fuels
- **Noise reduction**: achieved through chevron nozzles and acoustic liners in modern engines
