## Tundra Region
*   Characterized by a polar climate with an average annual temperature of -12°C to -6°C and less than 25 cm (10 inches) of precipitation per year.
*   Underlain by permafrost, a layer of permanently frozen soil that can extend hundreds of meters deep and only thaws in the top 10-50 cm during the brief summer.
*   Supports low biodiversity with flora primarily consisting of low-lying shrubs, sedges, mosses, and lichens; no deep-rooted plants or trees can survive the frozen ground.
*   Experiences two main seasons: a short, cool growing season of 50-60 days and a long, harsh winter with near-constant darkness for several months.
*   Divided into two types: the Arctic tundra encircling the North Pole and the Alpine tundra found at high elevations on mountains worldwide, above the tree line.

## Core Ideas
- Permafrost underlies 85% of the tundra, reaching depths of 450–900 meters and remaining frozen for at least two consecutive years.
- The region experiences an extremely short growing season of 50–60 days and average annual temperatures between -12°C and -6°C.
- Vegetation is dominated by low-lying, hardy species like mosses, lichens, sedges, and dwarf shrubs, all under 15 cm tall to minimize wind exposure.
- Fauna is highly specialized for cold, including species like the Arctic fox, whose fur provides insulation down to -50°C, and migratory caribou herds that can exceed 100,000 individuals.
- The tundra acts as a massive carbon sink, storing an estimated 1,400 gigatons of carbon within its frozen soil, making it critical to the global climate system.

## How To Learn
- **Engage in active recall**: After reading a section on permafrost, close your book and write down 3–5 key facts, such as its depth (up to 1,500 meters in Siberia) and active layer thickness (0.3–1 meter).
- **Use spaced repetition**: Schedule reviews of tundra-specific terms (e.g., thermokarst, solifluction) at increasing intervals: 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, and 1 month after initial study.
- **Create concept maps**: Visually link abiotic factors like average annual temperature (-12°C to -6°C) and precipitation (15–25 cm/year) to their impacts on flora (e.g., dwarf shrubs) and fauna (e.g., migratory caribou).
- **Apply through case studies**: Analyze real data, such as the 1.2°C temperature increase in the Alaskan tundra over 30 years, to predict effects on carbon storage and species distribution.

## Examples
- **Permafrost Depth**: Continuous permafrost extends 400-700 meters deep in northern Alaska, with active layer thaw of 15-50 cm annually.
- **Plant Adaptations**: Arctic willow (*Salix arctica*) grows prostrate, under 10 cm tall, with shallow roots to avoid frost heave and conserve heat.
- **Animal Specializations**: Lemming populations cycle every 3-4 years, with winter nest insulation reaching 25°C warmer than ambient temperatures.
- **Carbon Storage**: Tundra soils hold approximately 1,600 gigatons of organic carbon—nearly twice the carbon in the atmosphere.

## Summary
- Covers approximately 20% of Earth's land surface, primarily in the Arctic Circle, northern Canada, Russia, and Alaska.
- Characterized by permafrost, a layer of permanently frozen subsoil extending up to 450 meters deep in some areas.
- Experiences extreme temperatures, with winter averages of -34°C and a short summer growing season of just 50-60 days.
- Supports low biodiversity with only about 1,700 species of vascular plants and iconic fauna like caribou, Arctic fox, and musk ox.
- Highly vulnerable to climate change, with current warming rates approximately twice the global average.
